How to read this paper

Ten guided stops through Liang (2025). Each stop has the move the author is making, the passage to read, and one thing to do with your hands.

This is a methods-and-pedagogy paper that hides three sharper claims: (a) Urban AI is its own thing, not a flavour of GIS; (b) judgement matters more than coding skill; (c) data centers are a planning problem and most curricula ignore this. Read with those in mind.

📖

1 Three learning goals

Abstract & Introduction · what the paper is trying to do

The paper opens with a deceptively simple list. Three meta learning goals for an Urban AI course:

"...three meta learning goals: applying AI effectively and appropriately in urban challenges, addressing its social, environmental, and governance impacts, and developing normative judgements and professional identities around AI."

Most existing tech-in-planning courses cover the first two. The third — professional identity — is the new move and it is where the paper gets its argument from. Watch how this third goal keeps returning later as the justification for reflection journals and the criticality rubric.

2 Why Urban AI is its own thing

Literature Review · the disciplinary boundary work

This is where the author has to defend the existence of the subfield. The argument is structural, not just rhetorical: Urban AI engages multimodal data, socio-technical complexity, and infrastructure (data centers) that adjacent fields explicitly don't.

"Different from Geography and Data Science, urban planning engages a broader range of multimodal data (texts, graphics, transactions) and domain-specific challenges (e.g., zoning code analysis, generative design, digital twins). Standard AI models are often ill-equipped to handle urban planning challenges because cities are complex, dynamic socio-technical systems... Urban Planning also extends further into the governance, socio-politics, and the built environment consequences of AI in urban life. A prominent example is permitting the land, energy, and water uses of data centers..."
Hover the highlights.Each is a deliberate distinction from a neighbouring field.

The clearest tell: data centers. If your discipline doesn't already treat the siting and permitting of AI infrastructure as a first-class problem, it isn't doing Urban AI.

3 The five pedagogical tensions

Pedagogical Tension in Urban AI · the unavoidable tradeoffs

This section is the cleanest part of the paper. Five tensions are introduced, each as a binary that can't be avoided. The author isn't trying to resolve them; she's mapping them so other instructors can take positions deliberately.

The five tensions are: Breadth vs Depth · Literacy vs Capacity · Technique-centered vs Context-centered · Big Tech vs Alternatives · Learning about AI vs Learning with AI.

4 What the pilot course actually looked like

Pilot Course Context · the empirical setting

Twenty students, mostly Master's in Planning and BS in Urban Tech, all with stats and one programming course completed. A 16-week semester. Most weeks: a lecture paired with a Python lab. Weekly reflection journals were the assessment backbone.

"Most weeks consisted of a lecture paired with a hands-on Python lab. ... Beyond OpenAI's models, the course introduced students to the Hugging Face open-source model ecosystem... Students were encouraged to use AI tools freely throughout the course but were required to submit weekly reflection journals (12 out of 16 weeks)..."

5 How 235 journals became evidence

Research Methods · the qualitative coding pipeline

The paper's evidentiary backbone is 655 quotes coded from 235 reflection journals. The coding ran in three rounds with ChatGPT-4o as a human-in-the-loop assistant — a methodologically interesting choice for a paper that's also critiquing AI in workflows.

By the numbers
20students enrolled
235journals analysed
655quotes coded
40distinct categories
1
Manual round: identify text chunks, capture emerging themes. AI deliberately excluded to avoid label bias.
2
Refine round: AI suggests category names from quote clusters. Author confirms every assignment.
3
Define round: AI generates per-level definitions. Author runs full sanity check; every quote traceable in ATLAS.ti.
The defence the author offers is striking: she uses AI on a study that critiques AI, treats it strictly as "thought partner, not decision maker," and keeps a full audit trail. This is itself a Level-4 demonstration of the rubric the paper proposes.

Modules 1–4 form a deliberate progression: how AI works → how it applies to planning → how it should be governed → how it reshapes urban systems. Module 5 runs across all of them as a reflection layer — the place where the third learning goal (identity) gets practised.

8 The criticality framework

9 What students actually did

Findings · Figure 2 reconstructed

The heatmap in Figure 2 is the empirical heart of the paper. Reading across: the most common use was Understanding, Reasoning, and Expression — the cognitive work of learning. Reading up: the categories that reached higher criticality were Domain Application and reflections on Effective and Appropriate Use in Context.

Reconstructed heat — application categories × criticality

Frequencies from journal coding
The key pattern — domain application appears less often but the few instances that exist cluster at higher criticality. That's the paper's evidence for "context-sensitive use produces deeper thinking."

"High criticality emerges not from the most technically intensive uses, but from context-sensitive applications and reflections that connect AI to planning judgement and responsibility."

The other empirical surprise: 62% of all AI tool instances reported were ChatGPT, despite an explicit Hugging Face curriculum. The Big Tech tension (the third one in section 3) is not theoretical.

The takeaway

The paper closes with a constraint that's easy to miss: Urban AI is best taught as the last piece of the technical sequence. Not the first. You teach evidence-based planning and data governance first — because the whole point of Urban AI is judgement, and judgement needs something to judge against.
