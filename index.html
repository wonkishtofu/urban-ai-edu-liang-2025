What should urban planners learn
in the age of AI?

A pilot course at Michigan tried to answer this. The findings reshape what counts as expertise in planning — and the answer is not "more coding."

Xiaofan Liang taught twenty students an Urban AI course in 2025 and coded 655 quotes from 235 reflection journals to figure out what worked. The paper is dense; the ideas underneath are simple and they have teeth. This page lets you turn them over in your hands.

"High criticality emerges not from the most technically intensive uses, but from context-sensitive applications and reflections that connect AI to planning judgement and responsibility."

1 · Urban AI is not Urban Informatics, GIS, or GeoAI

Planning has absorbed a generation of computational tools — but Urban AI is a different kind of object. It is not just another analytical layer on top of GIS. It is a socio-technical force that changes who gets to make decisions, what counts as expertise, and what infrastructure cities are obliged to host.

Click each field to see how it positions itself across four dimensions.

Each field rates from low to high on what it foregrounds. Urban Informatics is heavy on data wrangling; GeoAI fuses ML with maps; Urban AI uniquely foregrounds governance, ethics, and professional identity.

2 · The Criticality Matrix

The headline contribution of the paper is a 2-axis rubric for student work. One axis is agency — what you do with AI. The other is reflection — how deeply you think about that doing. A student can be a strong coder (high agency) and still operate at Level 1 reflection. A non-technical student can reach Level 4 reflection by asking the right ethical question.

Click any cell. The matrix is a map of possible student states — and a diagnostic for which kind of growth a course is actually producing.

Liang's empirical finding, when she coded all 655 quotes: students cluster at low reflection levels even when they're technically capable. Governance and ethics topics appeared in only ~10% of reflections. The matrix is a tool for noticing this gap and pulling students up the y-axis on purpose.

3 · Five tensions you cannot escape

Every course design is a position taken on five tradeoffs. Slide each one to see what the course becomes at that setting. There is no "correct" position — but there is no neutral position either.

Liang's pilot leaned right on most of these: depth, capacity-building, context-centered, with deliberate exposure to open-source alternatives. The hardest tension in her data was Big Tech vs alternatives — students used ChatGPT for 62% of all AI interactions despite a Hugging Face curriculum.

A small town's worth of water. A small city's worth of power. A small farm-belt's worth of land. Planners — not engineers — decide whether this happens.

U.S. data-center electricity consumption is projected to double or triple by 2028. The choice of where they get built, what they're allowed to draw from, and who bears the externality is — by jurisdiction — a planner's job. The paper argues this is now part of Urban AI literacy.

7 · The Level-5 planner

The top of the matrix isn't a coder. It's someone who can move fluidly between technical work and policy stance — who builds AI and knows when to refuse to. From the paper:

Three signature moves of a Level-5 planner
1. Builds with intent Designs or meaningfully customises AI pipelines for a planning-specific problem (e.g. a civic pothole-reporter that routes by neighbourhood equity, not just spatial proximity).
2. Strategises across scale Aligns the deployment with public values, environmental footprint, and institutional capacity. Knows when the AI is the answer and when the AI is the wrong question.
3. Synthesises new roles Articulates a position on what AI should mean for planning as a profession — and acts on it. Mentors, writes policy, refuses procurements.

One of Liang's groups designed a chatbot audit that would have flooded a city service with thousands of automated queries. They figured out it would work technically — and then chose not to do it. That is the curriculum's deepest success: the student who picks up the tool and puts it down again, on purpose.

8 · So what?

If you teach planning: this paper gives you a 16-week scaffold, a rubric, and 235 journals' worth of evidence about how students actually engage. The criticality matrix is the most portable artefact — it works in any AI-adjacent course.

If you practice planning: the paper is telling you the next decade's hiring will reward people who can govern AI, not people who can run it. The data center file on your desk is already this conversation.

If you build AI: the failure modes Liang describes (zoning heterogeneity, contingent open models, political reality of deployment) are not user-error. They are signals that the system needs domain partners, not better fine-tuning.

Urban AI is the last piece of the technical sequence, not the first. You teach it after evidence-based planning and data governance — because the point is judgement, and judgement needs something to judge against.
